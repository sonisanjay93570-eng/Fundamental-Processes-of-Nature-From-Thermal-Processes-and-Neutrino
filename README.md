Abstract

Nature contains a wide range of physical, chemical, and biological processes. Elementary particles interact, atomic nuclei form, electrons become bound to nuclei, atoms participate in chemical reactions, and complex biological systems maintain organized activity through continuous exchanges of matter and energy.

This research model proposes a common conceptual framework for examining relationships among these processes.

The framework begins with the thermal conditions of the early universe and the interactions among elementary particles. It considers the formation of protons and neutrons, the role of neutrinos in weak interactions, the formation of light atomic nuclei, the emergence of neutral atoms, the development of chemical complexity, and the organization of biological systems.

The central idea of Action-Flow Theory is that physical structures and observable activities can be investigated through interactions, energy transfer, changes in state, and the resulting organization of matter.

The model does not claim that all these processes are governed by one newly discovered mechanism. Instead, it proposes a research framework for examining how established physical, chemical, and biological mechanisms relate to one another.

Particular attention is given to the role of neutrinos in the early universe. Neutrinos are electrically neutral particles that interact primarily through the weak interaction and gravity. Their interactions influenced the neutron-to-proton ratio in the early universe and therefore contributed indirectly to the conditions governing primordial light-element formation.

The model invites researchers to evaluate its conceptual structure, identify established mechanisms, develop quantitative relationships, and determine whether it offers useful questions or predictions beyond existing scientific explanations.

1. Introduction

The universe contains matter organized at many levels, from elementary particles to atomic nuclei, atoms, molecules, stars, planets, and living organisms.

These levels are connected, but the physical mechanisms responsible for their formation are not identical.

Quarks and gluons participate in strong interactions. Neutrinos participate in weak interactions. Electromagnetic interactions govern atomic structure and most chemical bonding. Gravity contributes to the formation of large astronomical structures.

Biological systems depend on complex chemical reactions, energy transformations, molecular organization, and regulatory mechanisms.

The central research question is:

Can these different processes be examined within a common conceptual framework based on interactions, energy transfer, changes in physical state, and the formation of new structures?

Action-Flow Theory approaches this question by examining processes rather than treating observable structures as isolated objects.

The framework does not replace established theories. It seeks to organize questions about how different physical processes connect and how their resulting structures make subsequent processes possible.

3. The Central Concept of Action-Flow Theory

Action-Flow Theory is a process-based conceptual framework developed by Sanjay Soni.

Its starting point is the observation that physical and biological systems undergo changes through interactions and exchanges of energy and matter.

For research purposes, the framework uses the following conceptual sequence:

Initial Conditions → Interactions → Energy and Momentum Transfer → Change in State → New Organization → Subsequent Processes

This sequence is a general way of organizing observations. It does not mean that every physical or biological process follows one identical mechanism.

For example:

- A photon may transfer energy to an electron.
- A weak interaction may change a neutron into a proton under suitable conditions.
- Electromagnetic interactions allow electrons to become bound to nuclei.
- Chemical reactions rearrange atoms and their bonds.
- Cellular processes use chemical energy to support transport, synthesis, signaling, and regulation.

Each process requires its own established physical laws and mathematical description.

The proposed contribution of Action-Flow Theory is to investigate whether a shared process-based description can help researchers compare these different levels without confusing their underlying mechanisms.

4. Thermal Conditions and the Early Universe

The early universe was much hotter and denser than it is today.

Its physical conditions changed as the universe expanded. As the temperature decreased, different particles and structures became possible or stable under the prevailing conditions.

At sufficiently high temperatures, matter existed in forms that differ substantially from ordinary matter today.

During the early stages, the universe contained interacting particle populations that included quarks, gluons, electrons, positrons, photons, neutrinos, and other particles, depending on the temperature and epoch.

As the universe expanded and cooled, important changes occurred:

- Quarks became confined within hadrons, including protons and neutrons.
- The rates of different particle interactions changed.
- Neutrinos gradually decoupled from the electromagnetic plasma.
- Neutrons and protons participated in processes determining the formation of primordial light nuclei.
- Much later, electrons became bound to nuclei, allowing neutral atoms to form.

These developments are established parts of modern cosmology, although research continues into several details of the early universe.

Research question

How can the temperature, expansion rate, particle populations, and interaction rates be quantitatively connected to the sequence of structures that emerged?

Connection to Action-Flow Theory

The framework treats thermal conditions as important variables that influence which interactions occur, how frequently they occur, and which resulting states are physically possible.

This is a conceptual organization of established physics, not a substitute for the equations of cosmology and particle physics.

5. Plasma, Thermal Energy, and Particle Interactions

A plasma is an ionized state of matter containing charged particles, such as free electrons and positively charged ions.

However, the early universe should not be described simply as a plasma made of neutrinos.

Neutrinos are electrically neutral and interact much more weakly with matter than charged particles do. They can be present in the same early-universe environment without being ordinary charged constituents of the plasma.

At sufficiently high temperatures, the early universe contained many interacting particle species. Their behavior depended on their masses, interaction strengths, energy distributions, and the expansion of the universe.

As conditions changed, some interactions became less frequent relative to the rate of cosmic expansion.

This distinction is important because the presence of a particle in an environment does not automatically mean that the particle determines every process occurring in that environment.

Research questions

1. How did thermal conditions influence the interaction rates of different particle species?
2. Under what conditions did particular interactions become less frequent?
3. How did these changes influence the subsequent evolution of matter?
4. Which aspects of this evolution can be described by a common conceptual framework, and which require separate physical mechanisms?

6. Neutrinos and Their Role in the Early Universe

Neutrinos are electrically neutral elementary particles.

They interact primarily through the weak nuclear interaction and gravity. Because their interaction probability with ordinary matter is usually very small, enormous numbers of neutrinos can pass through matter without interacting.

Their weak interaction does not mean that their effects are always negligible. In the early universe, the high temperature and density made weak interactions important for certain particle-conversion processes.

One important example is the conversion between neutrons and protons.

A representative reaction involving an electron neutrino is:

νₑ + n → p + e⁻

Here:

- νₑ represents an electron neutrino.
- n represents a neutron.
- p represents a proton.
- e⁻ represents an electron.

This reaction is possible when the relevant energy and kinematic conditions are satisfied.

Other weak-interaction reactions also contributed to neutron-proton conversion in the early universe.

As the universe expanded and cooled, these reactions became progressively less effective at maintaining neutron-proton equilibrium.

The resulting neutron-to-proton ratio influenced the abundance of primordial light elements, especially helium-4.

Important scientific distinction

Neutrinos did not independently create all protons, neutrons, atomic nuclei, or atoms.

The roles of different interactions must be distinguished:

- Strong interactions bind quarks within protons and neutrons.
- Weak interactions allow certain particle conversions and influence neutron-proton balance.
- Strong nuclear interactions bind protons and neutrons into atomic nuclei.
- Electromagnetic interactions bind electrons to nuclei and govern chemical bonding.

Research hypothesis

Action-Flow Theory proposes examining these interactions as successive, connected processes whose outcomes help establish the conditions for subsequent forms of organization.

The scientific question is whether this organization offers useful explanatory or predictive value beyond the existing theories.

7. From Elementary Particles to Atomic Nuclei

Protons and neutrons are composite particles made of quarks bound by gluons through the strong interaction.

Protons and neutrons can subsequently form atomic nuclei under suitable conditions.

During Big Bang nucleosynthesis, which began within the first several minutes after the Big Bang, nuclear reactions produced primarily hydrogen nuclei, helium nuclei, and small amounts of other light nuclei.

The formation of these nuclei depended on several factors:

- The expansion rate of the universe.
- The temperature and density of the early universe.
- The neutron-to-proton ratio.
- Nuclear reaction rates.
- The time available for nuclear reactions before the universe cooled further.

Neutrino-related weak interactions influenced some of these conditions, particularly the neutron-to-proton ratio.

However, the formation of nuclei was not caused by neutrinos alone.

Conceptual sequence

Quarks and Gluons → Protons and Neutrons → Nuclear Reactions → Light Atomic Nuclei

This sequence describes distinct physical stages. Each stage requires its own scientific explanation.

Research question

Can the framework clearly distinguish the processes that form particles from those that bind particles into larger structures?

8. From Atomic Nuclei to Neutral Atoms

An atomic nucleus contains protons and, in most nuclei heavier than ordinary hydrogen, neutrons.

Electrons can become bound to nuclei through electromagnetic interactions.

A neutral hydrogen atom contains one proton and one electron.

A neutral helium-4 atom contains a nucleus with two protons and two neutrons, together with two bound electrons.

The early universe initially remained too hot for stable neutral atoms to exist in abundance. As expansion lowered the temperature, electrons became bound to nuclei in a process commonly called recombination.

This occurred hundreds of thousands of years after the Big Bang, long after the initial formation of light nuclei.

Conceptual sequence

Atomic Nucleus + Bound Electrons → Neutral Atom

The formation of a neutral atom is different from the formation of its nucleus.

Nuclear processes and electromagnetic binding must therefore be treated as separate stages within the model.

Research question

How can the framework represent the transition from a nuclear structure to an atomic structure without treating the transition as a single undifferentiated event?

9. From Atoms to Molecules and Chemical Complexity

Atoms can interact through electromagnetic processes involving their electrons and nuclei.

Under suitable conditions, these interactions produce chemical bonds and molecules.

For example:

H + H → H₂

Two hydrogen atoms can form a hydrogen molecule when the physical conditions permit the required energy transfer and the formation of a bound molecular state.

Other atoms can form a wide range of molecules and chemical compounds.

Chemical reactions rearrange atoms and their bonds. They do not ordinarily create new atomic nuclei.

Nuclear reactions, by contrast, change atomic nuclei and may transform one element into another.

This distinction is essential for a scientifically accurate model.

Factors influencing chemical development

- Temperature.
- Pressure.
- Concentration of reactants.
- Availability of energy.
- Molecular structure.
- Reaction pathways and activation barriers.
- Environmental conditions and catalysts.

These factors influence which chemical reactions occur and how quickly they proceed.

Connection to Action-Flow Theory

The framework examines chemical development as a sequence of interactions and structural changes in which existing molecular arrangements can produce different arrangements and properties.

This does not imply that all chemical reactions move toward greater complexity. Some produce simpler substances, some establish equilibria, and some release energy while others require energy input.

Research question

Can a process-based framework compare chemical transformations while retaining the quantitative laws that govern each reaction?

10. Physical Development and Chemical Development

Physical and chemical processes are closely related, but they are not identical.

Physical processes include changes in motion, temperature, pressure, phase, and the arrangement of matter.

Chemical processes involve changes in molecular composition and chemical bonding.

Nuclear processes can change the composition of atomic nuclei.

These distinctions allow the model to examine how one level of organization provides conditions for another.

For example:

- The formation of atomic nuclei establishes which elements can exist.
- The formation of atoms establishes the electronic structures that determine chemical behavior.
- Chemical bonding creates molecules with properties different from those of their separate constituent atoms.
- Chemical reactions can produce increasingly complex molecular systems under suitable conditions.

The history of the universe also includes the formation of stars, where nuclear reactions produced many elements heavier than those formed abundantly during Big Bang nucleosynthesis.

Stellar evolution, stellar explosions, and other astrophysical processes helped distribute these elements into the environments from which later generations of stars, planets, and other structures formed.

Conceptual sequence

Nuclear Structure → Atomic Structure → Chemical Bonding → Molecular Organization → Complex Matter

This sequence is a broad organizing framework. It does not imply that every stage occurs immediately after the preceding one or that complexity increases in every process.

11. Natural Conditions, Dynamic Balance, and Biological Activity

Nature contains systems that exchange matter and energy with their surroundings.

Some systems approach thermodynamic equilibrium. Others remain far from equilibrium because energy and matter continuously flow through them.

The distinction matters because natural organization does not require every system to be in a state of equilibrium.

For example, Earth receives energy from the Sun and releases energy into space. This energy flow supports atmospheric circulation, weather, and many chemical and biological processes.

Living organisms are also open systems.

They exchange matter and energy with their environments and use chemical reactions to maintain organized activity.

Examples include:

- Cellular metabolism.
- Transport across biological membranes.
- Protein synthesis.
- Ion movement across cell membranes.
- Neural signaling.
- Regulation of temperature and internal chemical conditions.

Biological homeostasis

Homeostasis is the regulation of internal conditions within ranges compatible with normal biological function.

It is a dynamic process rather than absolute stillness.

When conditions change, biological systems may respond through chemical, electrical, mechanical, and regulatory processes.

The resulting activity depends on the structure and capabilities of the organism.
Connection to Action-Flow Theory

The framework proposes examining biological activity through interactions, transfers of matter and energy, responses to changing conditions, and the resulting physiological states.

However, biological organization cannot be explained merely by stating that energy flows through matter. Biological mechanisms, molecular organization, evolution, and the conditions for the origin of life require specific explanations.

12. The Origin of Biological Organization

The transition from non-living chemistry to the earliest forms of life remains an active area of scientific research.

Research examines how non-living chemical systems may have developed characteristics associated with life, including:

- Compartmentalization.
- Chemical networks.
- Information storage.
- Catalysis.
- Energy use.
- Reproduction or replication.
- Heritable variation and evolution.

No single complete pathway from early non-living chemistry to the first living systems has been conclusively established.

It is therefore important not to treat chemical complexity alone as proof that life must emerge.

Proposed research question

Can the Action-Flow framework help organize the relationships among environmental energy, chemical reaction networks, molecular organization, and the emergence of systems capable of maintaining and reproducing their organization?

This is a question for investigation, not an established conclusion.

14. The Role of Energy, Motion, and Interaction
Energy is a measurable physical quantity. Motion describes changes in position or physical state, depending on the context. An interaction is a physical process through which systems influence one another.
The relationship among these concepts varies across different processes.
For example:
In electromagnetic interactions, energy and momentum may be transferred between radiation and matter.
In weak interactions, particles can change identity while satisfying applicable conservation laws.
In chemical reactions, the arrangement of atoms and bonds changes.
In biological systems, chemical energy supports processes such as transport, synthesis, and movement.
Energy is conserved in accordance with the relevant physical laws, although the precise formulation depends on the system and its spacetime context.
The phrase “energy flow” is used in this model as a conceptual description of energy transfer. It should not be interpreted as a new physical quantity or an independent force.
Research objective
To determine whether the language of energy transfer, interaction, and state change can help compare these processes without replacing the mathematical descriptions already established in physics, chemistry, and biology.

Energy and momentum transfer.
Reaction rate.
Chemical concentration.
Pressure.
Measurable biological response.
Not every variable applies to every process. A research question must specify the relevant system and conditions.
Stage 3: Develop a quantitative model
The researcher should identify a particular relationship to calculate.
For example, under appropriate assumptions, the mean free path of a particle in a uniform target is:
λ = 1/(nσ)
where:
λ is the mean free path.
n is the target number density.
σ is the relevant interaction cross-section.
This relation illustrates how particle density and interaction probability affect the expected distance between interactions.
For a homogeneous target with a constant cross-section and independent interactions, the probability of at least one interaction over a path length L is:
P = 1 − exp(−L/λ)
These equations are established relations, not new equations proposed by Action-Flow Theory.
A model-specific prediction would require additional assumptions and a clearly specified physical system.
Stage 4: Compare predictions with existing evidence
Where possible, compare calculated results with:
Established experimental measurements.
Published observational data.
Validated physical simulations.
Known chemical reaction rates.
Biological measurements relevant to a specified hypothesis.
A conceptual diagram alone cannot establish a new physical mechanism.
Stage 5: Design suitable experiments
Experiments should be conducted only where the necessary equipment, safety procedures, and scientific expertise are available.
A simple chemical experiment may be suitable for studying temperature and reaction rates.
Neutrino interactions, however, require specialized detectors, appropriate background controls, and expert analysis. They cannot be demonstrated reliably through ordinary household experiments.
Stage 6: Independent verification
Other researchers should be able to examine the methods, assumptions, data, calculations, and conclusions.
The model should be revised when evidence reveals errors, missing mechanisms, or incorrect assumptions.
17. Limitations of the Current Model
This model is an initial conceptual framework and has several limitations.
It does not introduce a new experimentally established physical law.
It does not provide a complete mathematical description of every transition in the proposed sequence.
It does not claim that neutrinos are the constituents of ordinary plasma.
It does not claim that neutrinos independently create all matter, atomic nuclei, or atoms.
It does not treat chemical reactions and nuclear reactions as the same process.
It does not claim that natural balance alone explains the origin of life.
It does not establish that one universal mechanism governs all physical, chemical, and biological phenomena.
It does not replace established theories such as quantum mechanics, particle physics, thermodynamics, chemistry, cosmology, or biology.
These limitations are stated to make the research proposal more transparent and easier to evaluate.
